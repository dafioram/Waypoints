LLA and ECEF Coordinates

A GPS receiver works in Earth-centred Cartesian coordinates (ECEF), but people read latitude, longitude and altitude (LLA). This page defines both, shows the math for converting each way with worked examples, explains the vocabulary (ellipsoid, WGS84, geoid), lists the GPS conventions that cause bugs, and untangles the several different things called “altitude”.

1. Overview: two ways to name a point

LLA locates a point relative to a smooth reference surface (an ellipsoid): geodetic latitude \(\varphi\), longitude \(\lambda\), and a height \(h\) above that surface. ECEF (Earth-Centred, Earth-Fixed) locates the same point with three Cartesian numbers \((X, Y, Z)\) in metres, measured from the Earth’s centre of mass along axes that rotate with the planet.

LLA (geodetic)ECEF (geocentric Cartesian)
Numbers\(\varphi\) latitude (°), \(\lambda\) longitude (°), \(h\) height (m)\(X, Y, Z\) (m)
Measured fromThe WGS84 ellipsoid surfaceThe Earth’s centre of mass; axes fixed to the rotating Earth
Good forPeople, maps, labels, databases of placesDistances, vectors, rotations, satellite math; no special cases at the poles or the ±180° line
Easy to get wrongDegrees vs radians, lat/lon order, which “height”, wrap-aroundLarge values (~6.4×106 m) need double precision; both ends must use the same datum

The straight-line (chord) distance between two ECEF points is simply \(\sqrt{\Delta X^2+\Delta Y^2+\Delta Z^2}\), which is why navigation software does its real work in ECEF and only converts to LLA for display.

Figure 1. Left: a cut through the meridian plane containing point P. The surface normal through S crosses the Z axis at Q, not at the centre O. Right: the same point seen from above the north pole.

The key idea. The Earth is flattened, not a sphere. Its equatorial radius is 6,378,137 m but its polar radius is 6,356,752.314 m, so the poles sit 21,384.686 m (about 21 km) closer to the centre. Latitude on a map is the angle of the local vertical (the surface normal) above the equatorial plane, not the angle seen from the centre. That is why the formulas below contain \(e^2\) and the radius \(N(\varphi)\) instead of plain trigonometry.

Why not just treat the Earth as a sphere?

You can, if 20 km errors are acceptable. Using a sphere means treating geodetic latitude \(\varphi\) as if it were the angle \(\psi\) seen from the centre. Over the whole globe the two differ by up to 0.1924° (at about 45.1° latitude), which is roughly 21 km of north–south position error. Section 4 shows the spherical special case ( \(e = 0\) ) and Section 2 tabulates \(\varphi\) against \(\psi\).

2. Definitions

These terms recur everywhere in GPS and mapping documentation. The “geoid” cards are the foundation for the altitude section later.

Reference ellipsoid

A smooth, mathematically defined surface made by spinning an ellipse about its short axis (an oblate spheroid). It is a geometric model of the Earth’s overall shape, fixed by two numbers: the semi-major (equatorial) axis \(a\) and the flattening \(f\), where \(f = (a-b)/a\) and \(b\) is the semi-minor (polar) axis.

Datum

An ellipsoid plus a precise statement of where it sits and how it is oriented relative to the real Earth. Latitude and longitude are meaningless without one: the same numbers in two different datums can point to places tens of metres apart (between the old NAD27 and NAD83 datums, about 10 to 100 m across the contiguous United States). A datum is what turns a surface into a coordinate system.

WGS84 the GPS datum

The World Geodetic System 1984, maintained by the US National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency, is the datum GPS broadcasts and the one you should assume for any bare “lat/lon” from a phone or receiver. Two constants define its ellipsoid; everything else is derived.

In the EPSG registry (the standard catalogue of coordinate systems) WGS84 appears as three related systems: EPSG:4326 (latitude, longitude), EPSG:4979 (latitude, longitude, ellipsoidal height) and EPSG:4978 (ECEF X, Y, Z). Those last two are exactly the LLA and ECEF forms this page converts between.

Realizations: WGS84 vs ITRF

A datum is an idea; a realization is the actual set of station coordinates that makes it usable. WGS84 has been re-realized several times, each named for the GPS week of the adjustment (G730, G873, G1150, G1674, G1762, G2139, G2296). They are tied to the International Terrestrial Reference Frame (ITRF), the scientific standard. For example, WGS84 (G1674) was aligned to ITRF2008 at epoch 2005.0 with the transformation set to zero.

The EPSG registry therefore treats plain “WGS 84” as a datum ensemble with a stated accuracy of 2 m: if you only say “WGS84” you cannot promise agreement better than that between realizations. For navigation and mapping at metre level this is irrelevant. For centimetre-level work, tectonic plates drift by centimetres per year, so you must name the realization and the epoch (or use ITRF directly).

Geoid

The surface of constant gravitational potential that best matches global mean sea level, extended in imagination under the continents. Gravity is perpendicular to it everywhere, which is what a plumb line or a levelled instrument measures. It is physical (set by mass distribution), so it is lumpy: relative to the WGS84 ellipsoid it ranges from about −107.0 m to +85.4 m in the EGM96 model. An ellipsoid is geometry; the geoid is physics.

Geoid undulation \(N_g\)

The height of the geoid above the ellipsoid at a given latitude and longitude, also called geoid height or geoid separation. It is positive where the geoid lies above the ellipsoid and negative where it lies below. It is not looked up by formula: it comes from a gravity model such as EGM96 or EGM2008, distributed as a grid of values.

Notation warning: many texts write N for both this and the prime-vertical radius \(N(\varphi)\). Here the radius is \(N(\varphi)\) and the undulation is always \(N_g\).

Mean sea level (MSL)

The average height of the sea surface over a long period (tides, waves and weather averaged out), measured by tide gauges. “Height above MSL” on land really means height above a vertical datum that is realized by levelling from tide gauges and is close to the geoid. Section 6 untangles the differences.

Geodetic vs geocentric latitude

Geodetic latitude \(\varphi\) is the angle between the equatorial plane and the ellipsoid normal. It is the latitude on maps and in GPS. Geocentric latitude \(\psi\) is the angle between the equatorial plane and the line from the Earth’s centre. They agree at the equator and the poles and differ in between:

\[ \tan\psi = (1-e^{2})\tan\varphi \]

ECEF axes

Origin: the Earth’s centre of mass. Z: along the IERS Reference Pole, effectively the direction of the north pole. X: from the centre through the point where the IERS Reference Meridian (essentially the Greenwich meridian, 0° longitude) crosses the equator. Y: completes a right-handed system, so it points to 90° east on the equator. The frame is bolted to the Earth, so a point fixed to the ground has constant ECEF coordinates (apart from slow tectonic drift).

3. GPS conventions in brief

  • Signs. Latitude is positive north, negative south, range \([-90^\circ , +90^\circ ]\). Longitude is positive east, negative west, usually \([-180^\circ , +180^\circ ]\) (some systems use \([0^\circ , 360^\circ )\)). The meridians +180° and −180° are the same line.
  • Datum. GPS positions are WGS84. A coordinate pair without a datum label is assumed to be WGS84, and for phone and receiver data that is almost always right.
  • Units. Degrees for display and storage, metres for heights and ECEF, but trig functions in nearly every language want radians. Convert once, at the boundary.
  • Order. People say “lat, lon”. The EPSG definition of EPSG:4326 is also latitude first, then longitude. But GeoJSON and KML store longitude first, and many libraries treat a point as (x, y) = (lon, lat). Mixing these up puts a point in the wrong place, often in the wrong hemisphere, with no error message.
  • Axes. ECEF follows the definition in Section 2: Z north, X through 0° N 0° E, Y through 0° N 90° E.
  • Heights. A receiver solves for position in ECEF, so its native height is the ellipsoidal height \(h\). Many outputs (NMEA, many apps) then report an MSL altitude instead. Always check which one you have. Section 6 is entirely about this.

What receivers and files actually report

The NMEA 0183 GGA sentence is the most common fix message. Receiver documentation (for example NovAtel’s) defines its altitude field as the antenna altitude above or below mean sea level, and gives a separate undulation field described as the relationship between the geoid and the WGS84 ellipsoid. Both are in metres. A made-up but self-consistent sentence for the Denver point, with the checksum computed by XOR:

$GPGGA,123519,3944.3520,N,10459.4180,W,1,08,0.9,1609.3,M,-17.0,M,,*40

The altitude field is 1609.3 (above MSL) and the separation field is −17.0 (geoid is 17.0 m below the ellipsoid). Section 6 uses exactly these two numbers.

Heads-up for GPX files. The GPX 1.0 manual defines <ele> only as “Elevation in meters of the point” and does not say whether that is relative to mean sea level or the ellipsoid. It does define a separate <geoidheight> as the height of the geoid (mean sea level) above the WGS-84 ellipsoid. Because the vertical reference of <ele> is not stated, treat it as ambiguous unless whatever produced the file documents it.

4. LLA → ECEF

This direction is a closed-form calculation: plug in and evaluate. First compute \(N(\varphi)\), the distance along the surface normal from the ellipsoid to the Z axis (the segment Q–S in Figure 1):

Step 1: prime-vertical radius of curvature

\[ N(\varphi) = \frac{a}{\sqrt{1 - e^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}} \]

Step 2: ECEF coordinates (\(h\) is height above the ellipsoid)

\[ \begin{aligned} X &= \bigl(N(\varphi) + h\bigr)\cos\varphi\,\cos\lambda \\ Y &= \bigl(N(\varphi) + h\bigr)\cos\varphi\,\sin\lambda \\ Z &= \bigl(N(\varphi)\,(1 - e^{2}) + h\bigr)\sin\varphi \end{aligned} \]

Read it as: \((N+h)\cos\varphi\) is the distance \(p\) from the Z axis, which \(\lambda\) then splits into \(X\) and \(Y\). The \(Z\) equation has \((1-e^2)\) because the normal crosses the Z axis not at the centre but at \(-e^{2}N\sin\varphi\) below it (point Q in Figure 1). On a sphere \(e = 0\), the factor vanishes and you recover ordinary spherical coordinates.

Use ellipsoidal height. The \(h\) in these equations must be height above the ellipsoid (HAE). Putting a mean-sea-level altitude here shifts the point along the vertical by the geoid undulation, tens of metres in many places. See Section 6.

Worked examples (3): equator, North Pole, New York City

Example A: the equator at the prime meridian, \(h = 0\). With \(\varphi = 0\), \(\sin\varphi = 0\) so \(N = a\). Then \(X = a\cdot 1\cdot 1\), \(Y = a\cdot 1\cdot 0\), \(Z = 0\):

\[ (X, Y, Z) = (\,6{,}378{,}137.000,\ 0.000,\ 0.000\,)\ \text{m} \]

Example B: the North Pole, \(h = 0\). Here \(\sin\varphi = 1\) and \(\cos\varphi = 0\), so \(N = a/\sqrt{1-e^2} = 6{,}399{,}593.6258\) m, which is larger than \(a\). The \(X\) and \(Y\) terms vanish and \(Z = N(1-e^2) = a\sqrt{1-e^2} = b\):

\[ (X, Y, Z) = (\,0,\ 0,\ 6{,}356{,}752.3142\,)\ \text{m}, \qquad \text{longitude is irrelevant at the pole} \]

In floating point, \(\cos 90^\circ \) is about 6×10−17, not zero, so a program prints \(X \approx\) 3.9×10−10 m instead of exactly 0. That is rounding noise, not an error.

Derivation: where \(N(\varphi)\) and the \(Z\) equation come from

Work in the meridian plane (Figure 1, left), using \(x\) for the distance from the Z axis and \(z\) for height above the equatorial plane. The ellipsoid cross-section is

\[ \frac{x^{2}}{a^{2}} + \frac{z^{2}}{b^{2}} = 1, \qquad b^{2} = a^{2}(1-e^{2}). \]

The slope of the tangent is \(dz/dx = -\dfrac{b^2}{a^2}\dfrac{x}{z}\), so the normal has slope \(\dfrac{a^2}{b^2}\dfrac{z}{x}\). The geodetic latitude \(\varphi\) is the angle of the normal above the horizontal, hence

\[ \tan\varphi = \frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\frac{z}{x} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = (1-e^{2})\,x\tan\varphi. \]

Substitute into the ellipse equation and use \(b^2 = a^2(1-e^2)\):

\[ \frac{x^{2}}{a^{2}}\Bigl[1 + (1-e^{2})\tan^{2}\varphi\Bigr] = 1 \ \Longrightarrow\ x = \frac{a\cos\varphi}{\sqrt{1 - e^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}} = N\cos\varphi, \qquad z = N(1-e^{2})\sin\varphi. \]

The normal makes angle \(\varphi\) with the horizontal, so the length of the normal from S to the Z axis is \(x/\cos\varphi = N\). That is the definition of \(N(\varphi)\) as the distance S–Q. Going a height \(h\) along the normal adds \(h\cos\varphi\) to \(x\) and \(h\sin\varphi\) to \(z\):

\[ x_P = (N+h)\cos\varphi,\qquad z_P = \bigl(N(1-e^{2})+h\bigr)\sin\varphi. \]

Finally, rotate about the Z axis by longitude \(\lambda\): \(X = x_P\cos\lambda\), \(Y = x_P\sin\lambda\), \(Z = z_P\). Where the normal meets the Z axis (point Q) is at \(z = z_S - N\sin\varphi = -e^{2}N\sin\varphi\), which explains the statement under the equations.

Why \(Z\) uses \(N(1-e^2)\) and not \(N\)

\(X\) and \(Y\) both come from the horizontal distance \(p = (N+h)\cos\varphi\), which is a clean multiple of \(N\). For \(Z\), the ellipse is squashed vertically by the factor \(b/a = \sqrt{1-e^2}\), and the normal-based radius \(N\) is longer than the geocentric radius. Working it through (see the derivation above) the surface point has \(z = N(1-e^2)\sin\varphi\), while the extra height \(h\) is added unscaled along the normal, giving \(N(1-e^2)+h\). A useful check: at the pole \(N(1-e^2)\) equals \(b\) exactly, because \(a\sqrt{1-e^2} = b\).

Special case: a spherical Earth

Set \(e = 0\). Then \(N = R\) for every latitude and the equations collapse to the textbook spherical-to-Cartesian form with radius \(r = R + h\):

\[ X = r\cos\varphi\cos\lambda,\qquad Y = r\cos\varphi\sin\lambda,\qquad Z = r\sin\varphi. \]

The inverse is \(r = \sqrt{X^2+Y^2+Z^2}\), \(\varphi = \arcsin(Z/r)\), \(\lambda = \operatorname{atan2}(Y,X)\), with no iteration. The price is accuracy: feeding geodetic latitudes into a sphere moves points by up to about 21 km, because the sphere’s latitude is really \(\psi\) (Section 2). Spheres are fine for rough distances, never for GPS.

5. ECEF → LLA

Longitude is easy, and the distance from the Z axis is easy:

\[ \lambda = \operatorname{atan2}(Y, X), \qquad p = \sqrt{X^{2}+Y^{2}} \]

Use atan2, never atan(Y/X), so every quadrant comes out right and \(X = 0\) does not divide by zero. The result is in \((-180^\circ , +180^\circ ]\).

Latitude and height are the hard part, because they are coupled. Rearranging the forward equations,

\[ p = (N + h)\cos\varphi, \qquad Z = \bigl(N(1-e^2) + h\bigr)\sin\varphi, \qquad N = N(\varphi). \]

To find \(h\) you need \(N\), which needs \(\varphi\); to find \(\varphi\) you need \(h\). The classic fix is to guess, then refine until the answer stops changing. It converges very quickly because \(e^2\) is tiny.

Fixed-point iteration

\[ \varphi_{0} = \operatorname{atan2}\!\bigl(Z,\ p\,(1-e^{2})\bigr) \quad\text{(the answer if } h = 0\text{)} \] \[ N_k = N(\varphi_k),\qquad h_k = \frac{p}{\cos\varphi_k} - N_k,\qquad \varphi_{k+1} = \operatorname{atan2}\!\Bigl(Z,\ p\Bigl(1 - \frac{e^{2}N_k}{N_k + h_k}\Bigr)\Bigr) \]

Repeat until \(|\varphi_{k+1}-\varphi_k|\,(N_k+h_k)\) is below your tolerance in metres.

Bowring’s method (one pass, no loop)

Bowring (1976) replaces the loop with a clever first estimate through the reduced latitude \(\beta\) (the latitude on the circle that circumscribes the meridian ellipse):

\[ \beta = \operatorname{atan2}(Z\,a,\ p\,b),\qquad \varphi = \operatorname{atan2}\!\bigl(Z + e'^{2}\,b\,\sin^{3}\beta,\ \ p - e^{2}\,a\,\cos^{3}\beta\bigr) \] \[ h = p\cos\varphi + Z\sin\varphi - a\sqrt{1 - e^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi} \]

For New York it returns 40.7128000000°, −74.006000°, 10.000000 m directly. Its accuracy depends on altitude. The worst round-trip position error found over a grid of latitudes (every 5° from pole to pole, three longitudes) was:

So it is accurate to a few millimetres or better through low-Earth-orbit altitudes, which covers everything from deep mines to aircraft and most satellites. At GPS orbit height it is off by 0.21 m, so use the loop there, or run one more correction pass. In my own check, PROJ 9.8.1 (via pyproj 3.8.0) showed the same roughly 0.2 m deviation from the iterative answer at that altitude, so a library’s inverse may share this property.

The height formula above is a rearrangement worth knowing: since \(p\cos\varphi + Z\sin\varphi = N(1-e^2\sin^2\varphi) + h\) and \(N(1-e^2\sin^2\varphi) = a\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2\varphi}\), it gives \(h\) without dividing by \(\cos\varphi\), which is why it survives at the poles.

Closed-form and other published solutions

Because the coupling reduces to a quartic equation in the latitude, exact non-iterative solutions exist. Vermeille (2002) solved the quartic with Ferrari’s method. Heikkinen (1982) gave closed formulas that Zhu (1994), in a comparison of conversion methods, calls the most accurate. On the iterative side, Sudano (1997) and Fukushima (1999) use Newton–Raphson-style iteration, and Bowring (1976) showed that a single iteration of his formulation is accurate enough near the surface (previous box). For almost every application, the loop above or Bowring’s method is easier to write, read and test, and fast enough: a handful of trig calls per point.

Edge cases: poles, the centre, the date line, negative heights
  • Poles. When \(p = 0\), longitude is undefined. atan2(0, 0) returns 0 in most languages; that is a convention, not a fact. The formula \(h = p/\cos\varphi - N\) divides by \(\cos 90^\circ = 0\) there and loses precision as \(\varphi \to 90^\circ \). Use the pole-safe form \(h = p\cos\varphi + Z\sin\varphi - a\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2\varphi}\); the reference code in the cheat sheet does, and returns \(\varphi = 90^\circ \), \(h = Z - b\) for \((0, 0, Z)\).
  • Near the Earth’s centre. Inside a region of a few tens of kilometres around the centre, a point has more than one “closest” surface point and latitude is not unique. No GPS user is there; do not spend time handling it.
  • The date line. atan2 returns values in \((-180^\circ ,180^\circ ]\). A point at exactly 180° may come back as +180° or −180°; both mean the same meridian. Normalize explicitly if you compare longitudes or build bounding boxes.
  • Negative heights. Perfectly valid. Mines, the Dead Sea shore and large parts of the ocean have negative orthometric height, and in many places even sea level has a negative ellipsoidal height (Section 6).
  • Precision. One degree of latitude is about 111 km, so seven decimal places of a degree (10−7°) is about 1 cm. Store coordinates as 64-bit floats; 32-bit floats resolve only about half a metre in ECEF at Earth radius.

6. Altitude: HAE, geoid, mean sea level and friends

“How high is it?” has at least five different correct answers, depending on what you measure from. A GPS receiver natively knows only one of them. Using the wrong one is among the most common bugs in mapping, aviation and drone software, and the errors are not small: from a few metres to over a hundred.

The heights, side by side

The key relationship

\[ h = H + N_g \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad H = h - N_g \]

Sanity-check the signs with Figure 2: where the geoid is above the ellipsoid (\(N_g > 0\)), a point sitting at sea level (\(H = 0\)) has \(h = N_g > 0\). This is exact for practical purposes. The ellipsoid normal and the true plumb line differ by a few arc-seconds, which changes the result by far less than a millimetre.

Which height do I need?

  • Converting to or from ECEF, 3D geometry, satellite math: \(h\), ellipsoidal height.
  • Telling a person how high a place is, comparing with map elevations or DEMs: \(H\), orthometric (MSL) height.
  • How far a vehicle is above whatever is beneath it: AGL, computed with \(H\) for both vehicle and terrain, or \(h\) for both.
  • Separation between aircraft or smooth short-term altitude change: pressure altitude (with a shared reference setting).

Example 1: “sea level” has a different GPS height everywhere

If you float on the sea surface, your orthometric height is \(H = 0\) by definition. Your GPS ellipsoidal height is whatever the geoid undulation is at that spot (EGM96 values, computed from the 5′ grid):

Sydney Harbour area33.87° S, 151.21° E+22.50.0+22.5

So a ship in the Indian Ocean low reads a GPS height of about −107 m while sitting right on the water, and a vessel in the Solomon Sea reads about +68 m. The geoid spans roughly −107 m to +85 m about the ellipsoid, a range of about 192 m. That is small beside the ellipsoid’s own 21 km flattening, which is why an ellipsoid is a good model, but large beside typical GPS precision.

Example 3: from a GPS fix to a mountain-style elevation

Using the Denver fix from Section 3 ($GPGGA,123519,3944.3520,N,10459.4180,W,1,08,0.9,1609.3,M,-17.0,M,,*40). Take a point 5,280 ft (1609.344 m) above mean sea level. EGM96 gives \(N_g = -16.990\) m at 39.7392° N, 104.9903° W, so

\[ h = H + N_g = 1609.344 + (-16.990) = 1592.354\ \text{m} \]

That is what a receiver working natively in WGS84 would solve for. When it prints an NMEA sentence it reports the MSL altitude (1609.3) and the separation (−17.0), so you can recover \(h\): \(h = 1609.3 + (-17.0) = 1592.3\) m. Going the other way, a bare ellipsoidal height needs a geoid lookup first: \(H = h - N_g\).

The sentence is illustrative, not recorded from a device. A real receiver uses its own built-in geoid model, which may differ from EGM96 by a fraction of a metre or more.

Example 4: height above ground, and a classic bug

A drone hovers 120 m above the ground at the Denver point. The terrain model (a DEM) stores elevations in MSL: the ground is 1609.344 m. The drone’s GPS reports ellipsoidal height. The correct bookkeeping:

QuantityValue
Ground, MSL (from DEM)1,609.344 m
Drone, MSL = ground + AGL1,729.344 m
Drone, GPS ellipsoidal height \(h = H + N_g\)1,712.354 m
Wrong: GPS height − DEM elevation (mixes HAE and MSL)103.01 m
Error−16.99 m (equals \(N_g\))

Here the naive subtraction reads 17 m too low. In Sydney, where \(N_g = +22.5\) m, the same mistake reads 22.5 m too high (a drone truly 120 m up, over ground at 58 m MSL, would show 142.5 m). The error is exactly the local undulation, and its sign flips with the geoid. That can mean a collision warning that never fires, or a false one.

Example 5: barometric altitude is something else again

A barometer measures air pressure, and converts it to height with a fixed model of the atmosphere (the International Standard Atmosphere). With the standard sea-level pressure \(p_0 = 1013.25\) hPa, temperature \(T_0 = 288.15\) K and lapse rate \(L = 0.0065\) K/m, the pressure altitude in the lower atmosphere is

\[ H_p = \frac{T_0}{L}\left[1 - \left(\frac{p}{p_0}\right)^{0.190263}\right] \qquad\bigl(T_0/L = 44{,}330.77\ \text{m}\bigr) \]
Station pressurePressure altitudeComment
1030 hPa−138.5 mA high-pressure day at sea level: an altimeter set to 1013.25 reads below zero (−454 ft)
1013.25 hPa0.0 mStandard sea level, by definition
1000 hPa+110.9 m
850 hPa+1457.3 mA familiar weather-map level
835 hPa+1602.3 mAbout what the standard atmosphere predicts at Denver’s elevation; actual pressure there varies with the weather

At the same physical place, pressure altitude changes by tens of metres from day to day as the weather changes, with no movement at all. Aircraft handle this by setting a local reference pressure (“QNH”) so the altimeter reads elevation above mean sea level near the airfield, and by flying “flight levels” on the standard setting above a transition altitude so everyone shares one scale. Barometric altitude is useful for change in height over minutes; it is not a geometric height, and it is not \(H\).

Common mistakes

  1. Feeding MSL height into the ECEF formula. If you pass \(H\) where the equations expect \(h\), the point moves along the vertical by \(|N_g|\). At the Denver point that is 17.0 m, in New York 32.8 m, and for a ship at the Indian Ocean low 107 m. Typical sources of MSL values: the NMEA altitude field, DEM tiles, map spot heights, a phone “elevation” display.
  2. Getting the sign of \(N_g\) backwards. \(H = h - N_g\). Adding instead of subtracting doubles the error: 34 m at Denver.
  3. Mixing HAE and MSL in a clearance or AGL calculation (Example 4).
  4. Thinking a negative ellipsoidal height is a bug. It is normal in much of the world: at the New York point, sea level itself is at \(h = -32.8\) m.
  5. Mixing geoid models or vertical datums. EGM96, EGM2008 and national models (for example the US GEOID models for NAVD88) do not give identical numbers. Convert with the model your data source used.
  6. Treating barometric altitude as MSL. It is only equal on a day when the real atmosphere matches the standard one.
EGM96 vs EGM2008, and how receivers get \(N_g\)

A geoid model is a set of spherical-harmonic coefficients of Earth’s gravity field, usually distributed as a grid of undulations. EGM96 (NIMA, NASA Goddard Space Flight Center and Ohio State University, 1996) is complete to degree and order 360 and was published on a 15′ grid. EGM2008 (NGA, 2008) is complete to degree and order 2159 (with extra coefficients to 2190) and was published on 2.5′ and 1′ grids, so it resolves features of roughly 10 km where EGM96 resolves tens of kilometres. The two models can therefore give different undulations at the same point; which one your device or data source used matters if you care about sub-metre agreement.

A receiver has no table lookup service; it stores a coarse grid in memory and interpolates. The numbers on this page come from a 5′ EGM96 grid with bilinear interpolation, which in the package I used is within 0.06 m of the cubic evaluation of the same model. Treat decimetres in the tables above as precision of the arithmetic, not guaranteed accuracy against the true geoid.

Vertical datums: what “MSL” really means on land (NAVD88 and friends)

You cannot measure mean sea level at a mountain, so orthometric heights on land are propagated inland from tide gauges by levelling (surveying height differences step by step) and corrected with gravity data. The resulting network is a vertical datum. Three examples:

  • NAVD88 (US, with Canada and Mexico; EPSG:5703 in the registry) came from a 1991 minimum-constraint adjustment of the Canadian–Mexican–US levelling, holding fixed the height of one primary tidal benchmark at Father Point/Rimouski, Quebec. NGS notes that mean sea level “is not the same equipotential surface at all tidal bench marks”, which is why only that one benchmark was held.
  • Ordnance Datum Newlyn (Great Britain) is based on about six years of mean sea level recorded at Newlyn, Cornwall, around 1915 to 1921.
  • Australian Height Datum (AHD71) follows approximate mean sea level at 30 tide gauges for 1966 to 1968, spread across the continent by levelling.

Such a datum is a realization of “sea level” fixed by particular benchmarks and tide gauges, so it will not coincide exactly with a global geoid model; expect a local offset and tilt whose size depends on the region and the model. In the US, NGS publishes GEOID models that convert ellipsoidal heights to NAVD88. For survey work, use the model the datum’s owner recommends rather than EGM96 or EGM2008. The EPSG registry also catalogues EGM96 and EGM2008 themselves as vertical systems (EPSG:5773 and EPSG:3855).

Sea-surface topography: why the sea is not exactly the geoid

The geoid is the level surface the ocean would have at rest. The real sea surface departs from it because of ocean currents, atmospheric pressure and similar processes. This dynamic topography is typically about ±1 m, roughly one-hundredth of the geoid undulations themselves. On top of that come tides, storm surge and a slow long-term rise in sea level. So “sea level = geoid” is a good working definition, accurate to about a metre locally, not an exact physical statement. That is one reason a floating ship’s GPS height will never match \(N_g\) to the centimetre.

Pre-flight checklist

  • Is the datum WGS84 (or whatever both ends agree on)?
  • Degrees converted to radians exactly once?
  • Latitude first or longitude first in this API?
  • Is each height ellipsoidal (\(h\)), MSL (\(H\)) or AGL? Is the source documented?
  • Does the ECEF radius fall between about 6,356,752 m and 6,378,137 m plus your height?
  • Does a round trip reproduce the input to the nanometre scale?
Reference code: JavaScript and Python (tested)

Both versions use the iteration from Section 5 with the pole-safe height formula. They were run against the same test points (equator, both poles, New York, Sydney, Everest’s coordinates, GPS-orbit altitude, a deep mine, a point beside the date line). The JavaScript and Python results agree to better than 10−8 m and round-trip to about 3×10−9 m or better.

JavaScript

// WGS84 geodetic <-> ECEF.  Degrees and metres.  Run with: node ref_lla.js
const A = 6378137.0;                 // semi-major axis (m)
const INV_F = 298.257223563;         // inverse flattening
const F = 1 / INV_F;
const E2 = F * (2 - F);              // first eccentricity squared
const rad = d => d * Math.PI / 180;
const deg = r => r * 180 / Math.PI;
const primeVertical = phi => A / Math.sqrt(1 - E2 * Math.sin(phi) ** 2);   // N(phi)

function llaToEcef(latDeg, lonDeg, h) {
  const phi = rad(latDeg), lam = rad(lonDeg), N = primeVertical(phi);
  return [
    (N + h) * Math.cos(phi) * Math.cos(lam),
    (N + h) * Math.cos(phi) * Math.sin(lam),
    (N * (1 - E2) + h) * Math.sin(phi),
  ];
}

function ecefToLla(x, y, z, tolMetres = 1e-9) {
  const p = Math.hypot(x, y);
  const lon = Math.atan2(y, x);
  let phi = Math.atan2(z, p * (1 - E2));          // first guess: assume h = 0
  let h = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < 20; i++) {
    const N = primeVertical(phi);
    // pole-safe form of h = p / cos(phi) - N
    h = p * Math.cos(phi) + z * Math.sin(phi) - A * Math.sqrt(1 - E2 * Math.sin(phi) ** 2);
    const next = Math.atan2(z, p * (1 - E2 * N / (N + h)));
    const moved = Math.abs(next - phi) * (N + h);   // metres
    phi = next;
    if (moved < tolMetres) break;
  }
  return [deg(phi), deg(lon), h];
}

if (typeof module !== "undefined") module.exports = { llaToEcef, ecefToLla };
if (typeof require !== "undefined" && require.main === module) {
  console.log(llaToEcef(40.7128, -74.0060, 10));        // [1334000.5447, -4654052.1292, 4138306.7614]
  console.log(ecefToLla(1334000.5446860685, -4654052.129206824, 4138306.7613728405));
}

Python

"""WGS84 geodetic <-> ECEF.  Degrees and metres."""
from math import radians, degrees, sin, cos, sqrt, atan2, hypot

A = 6378137.0                 # semi-major axis (m)
INV_F = 298.257223563         # inverse flattening
F = 1.0 / INV_F
E2 = F * (2.0 - F)            # first eccentricity squared


def prime_vertical(phi):
    return A / sqrt(1.0 - E2 * sin(phi) ** 2)          # N(phi)


def lla_to_ecef(lat_deg, lon_deg, h):
    phi, lam = radians(lat_deg), radians(lon_deg)
    n = prime_vertical(phi)
    return ((n + h) * cos(phi) * cos(lam),
            (n + h) * cos(phi) * sin(lam),
            (n * (1.0 - E2) + h) * sin(phi))


def ecef_to_lla(x, y, z, tol_m=1e-9):
    p = hypot(x, y)
    lon = atan2(y, x)
    phi = atan2(z, p * (1.0 - E2))                      # first guess: assume h = 0
    h = 0.0
    for _ in range(20):
        n = prime_vertical(phi)
        # pole-safe form of h = p / cos(phi) - N
        h = p * cos(phi) + z * sin(phi) - A * sqrt(1.0 - E2 * sin(phi) ** 2)
        nxt = atan2(z, p * (1.0 - E2 * n / (n + h)))
        moved = abs(nxt - phi) * (n + h)                # metres
        phi = nxt
        if moved < tol_m:
            break
    return degrees(phi), degrees(lon), h
Local frames: ENU and NED (brief)

To describe where something is relative to a reference point (an antenna, a drone home point), rotate the ECEF difference into a local East–North–Up (ENU) frame. With \(\varphi, \lambda\) the geodetic latitude and longitude of the reference point and \((X_0, Y_0, Z_0)\) its ECEF position:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} e \\ n \\ u \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -\sin\lambda & \cos\lambda & 0 \\ -\sin\varphi\cos\lambda & -\sin\varphi\sin\lambda & \cos\varphi \\ \cos\varphi\cos\lambda & \cos\varphi\sin\lambda & \sin\varphi \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} X - X_0 \\ Y - Y_0 \\ Z - Z_0 \end{bmatrix} \]

North–East–Down (NED), common in aerospace, is the same vector reordered as \((n, e, -u)\). Note that \(u\) is measured along the ellipsoid normal, so it is a height difference in the \(h\) sense.

8. Sources and how the numbers were made

How the numbers were made

  • Every ECEF, iteration and round-trip number comes from a short Python script using the two WGS84 defining constants; nothing was calculated by hand.
  • The forward conversion (LLA → ECEF) was cross-checked against PROJ 9.8.1 (pyproj 3.8.0, EPSG:4979 → EPSG:4978) at eight test points and matched exactly. The inverse matched to about 10−7 m except at GPS-orbit altitude (see the Bowring note).
  • Geoid undulations are EGM96 values from the 5′ grid bundled in the egm96 Python package (v0.3.0), which redistributes the GeographicLib grid and interpolates bilinearly. The “minimum” and “maximum” are grid extremes found by scanning that grid, not quoted from memory.
  • The NMEA sentence in Section 3 is illustrative; its checksum was computed in code.
