Realism

Philosophical realism is the general view that reality is not simply constituted by our thoughts, perceptions, language, or conceptual schemes. Things can be the way they are independently of whether anyone believes, perceives, or describes them. What counts as “realism,” however, varies considerably depending on what kind of reality is being defended.

The central realist intuition can be expressed simply: the world is not exhausted by our experience of it. A tree does not cease to exist when nobody sees it; a mathematical truth, a physical object, or perhaps even a moral fact may be thought to have a status that does not depend entirely on human minds.

Realism therefore tends to oppose positions that make reality fundamentally dependent on the subject. But realism is not necessarily the claim that we perceive reality exactly as it is. A philosopher can believe that there is a mind-independent world while also arguing that our access to that world is indirect, fallible, theory-laden, or structured by the conditions of human cognition.

The basic realist commitment

At its broadest, realism makes a distinction between what exists and what we think exists. The existence or nature of something need not be guaranteed by its being experienced, conceptualized, or named.

This creates two related but distinct questions:

  • Metaphysical realism: Is there a reality whose existence and structure are independent of our minds?
  • Epistemological realism: Can human beings acquire knowledge of that independent reality?

These questions should not be collapsed into one. Someone might answer “yes” to the first while being skeptical about the second. The world may exist independently of us even if our knowledge of it is incomplete or mediated by perception and conceptual interpretation.

Realism and the problem of appearance

One of realism's recurring problems is the gap between appearance and reality. We encounter the world through perception, yet perception can deceive us. A straight stick appears bent in water; distant objects appear smaller; colors can vary with lighting and observers.

The realist response is not necessarily to distrust perception altogether. Instead, realism can treat perceptual experience as evidence about a reality that exists beyond the experience itself. The difficulty is explaining how experience gives us access to that reality and how we distinguish genuine knowledge from systematic illusion.

René Descartes and realism

The Cartesian approach makes the problem especially sharp. Descartes begins by subjecting ordinary beliefs to radical doubt: perception can deceive, dreams can resemble waking experience, and even apparently obvious beliefs can be questioned.

The cogito establishes a point of certainty in the activity of thinking itself: while doubting, the thinker cannot coherently doubt that thinking is occurring. But this creates a new problem. Certainty about the existence of the thinking subject does not by itself establish the existence of a physical world outside the mind.

Descartes therefore represents an important route into realism rather than a simple statement of realism. The philosophical task becomes one of moving from certainty about thought toward justified belief in an external, mind-independent reality.

His distinction between primary and secondary qualities is also important for later realist discussions. Qualities such as extension, shape, and motion are treated as features of bodies themselves, while qualities such as color, taste, and sound are more closely connected to the way bodies affect perceiving subjects. This helps establish a recurring realist strategy: distinguish properties belonging to objects from features generated or mediated by our mode of experiencing them.

Major philosophers and realist ideas

Plato — reality beyond ordinary appearance

Plato's thought provides an early version of the distinction between appearance and a more fundamental reality. Sensible things are changeable and imperfect, while the Forms are presented as stable objects of knowledge.

In this sense, Plato's position can be understood as realist about Forms: mathematical, ethical, and other intelligible structures are not merely inventions of individual minds. What appears to us is not necessarily the deepest level of what is real.

Aristotle — realism about substances

Aristotle rejects the idea that Forms must exist as separate entities apart from particular things. His realism is instead closely tied to substances, their properties, causes, and structures.

On this view, the world we investigate is genuinely there to be known. Objects possess natures and properties independently of our descriptions of them. Knowledge can therefore be an achievement of discovering how things actually are rather than merely organizing appearances.

John Locke — primary and secondary qualities

Locke distinguishes between qualities that belong to bodies in a more objective sense and qualities that arise through the interaction between bodies and perceivers. Extension, shape, motion, and solidity are treated as primary qualities, while color, taste, smell, and similar features are secondary qualities.

This preserves a form of realism while acknowledging that perception does not simply reproduce objects in the mind exactly as they exist outside it. The external object is real, but our experience of its qualities depends in part on the relation between object and perceiver.

George Berkeley — an idealist challenge

Berkeley provides a major challenge to material realism. He argues against the notion of material substance existing independently of perception and famously connects the existence of sensible things with their being perceived.

His position demonstrates why realism cannot simply assume that an external object is philosophically obvious. If all that is directly available to us are experiences, what justifies the claim that there is a material substance existing behind those experiences?

Immanuel Kant — transcendental idealism and empirical realism

Kant complicates the realism-versus-idealism divide. He argues that objects as experienced are necessarily presented through structures contributed by the mind, including space, time, and the categories through which experience is organized.

At the same time, Kant does not simply reduce the experienced world to a private invention. He defends empirical realism: objects of ordinary experience are genuinely real within the framework of experience. His distinction between phenomena and things in themselves instead limits what human cognition can claim to know about reality independently of the conditions under which it appears to us.

Thomas Reid — common-sense realism

Reid objects to the idea that perception gives us only internal mental representations from which an external world must then be inferred. Ordinary perception, on his view, provides direct grounds for believing in external objects.

This becomes a form of common-sense realism: certain basic beliefs about the external world are not conclusions reached by elaborate philosophical inference. They are part of the natural operation of human cognition and provide the starting point for inquiry.

G. E. Moore — the ordinary world against skepticism

Moore develops a particularly direct response to radical skepticism. The existence of ordinary objects, such as hands and physical things, can be treated as more certain than some philosophical argument offered against them.

His approach reverses the burden of proof. Instead of assuming that philosophical skepticism has shown that the external world is doubtful, Moore asks why an abstract skeptical argument should outweigh the ordinary evidence for the existence of the world.

Karl Popper — realism and scientific theories

Popper's philosophy of science is compatible with scientific realism: scientific theories attempt to describe a world that exists independently of our theories. A theory is not merely a convenient instrument for organizing observations.

At the same time, scientific knowledge remains fallible. A successful theory can later be replaced by a better one. Realism therefore does not require that current scientific theories provide a final or perfectly accurate description of reality.

Hilary Putnam — realism without a God's-eye description

Putnam challenges a simple version of metaphysical realism according to which there is one completely determinate world whose structure can be described from an external, “God's-eye” standpoint.

His arguments emphasize the relationship between reality, reference, language, and conceptual schemes. This produces a more nuanced position: rejecting an absolute, perspective-free description of the world does not require accepting that reality is merely whatever we choose to say it is.

Variants of realism

Metaphysical realism

Metaphysical realism holds that reality, or at least some portion of it, exists independently of human minds and conceptual activity. The world does not depend for its existence on our believing in it.

The strongest versions may claim that reality has a determinate structure independently of every possible conceptual scheme. More modest versions simply insist that there are facts about the world that are not made true by our thoughts.

Epistemological realism

Epistemological realism concerns whether the independent world can be known. A realist need not claim that knowledge is infallible. Instead, the important idea is that beliefs can succeed or fail according to how things actually are.

This allows for degrees of knowledge, error, correction, and approximation. We can be wrong about reality precisely because reality is not simply determined by what we believe.

Scientific realism

Scientific realism extends the realist commitment to the entities and structures posited by successful scientific theories. Electrons, genes, fields, and other theoretical entities are treated as candidates for genuine features of the world rather than merely useful fictional devices.

The major challenge is the history of science: many theories once regarded as successful descriptions of reality were later rejected. Scientific realists therefore often emphasize that successive theories can become progressively better at describing an underlying reality without assuming that any particular theory is final.

Moral realism

Moral realism is the meta-ethical view that there are objective moral facts or values that exist independently of our beliefs, feelings, or cultural norms. According to this view, statements like "cruelty is wrong" are not just expressions of subjective preference, but truth-functional claims about the real world.

Mathematical realism (Platonism)

Mathematical realism posits that mathematical entities—such as numbers, sets, and geometric shapes—exist independently of the human mind. Under this view, mathematicians do not invent mathematics; they discover it.

Direct realism

Direct realism argues that perception puts us in genuine cognitive contact with external objects rather than giving us access only to internal representations.

This does not mean that perception is infallible. Rather, ordinary perceptual experience is understood as being about real objects in the world. Errors occur because the perceptual system can sometimes misrepresent those objects.

Critical realism

Critical realism combines belief in a mind-independent reality with a strong awareness of the limitations of human knowledge. Reality exists independently, but our descriptions of it are historically, culturally, conceptually, and perceptually mediated.

This position therefore occupies a middle ground between naive realism and radical idealism. We do not simply see reality exactly as it is, but neither does the mediation of perception imply that there is no reality beyond perception.

Common-sense realism

Common-sense realism treats ordinary beliefs about the external world as legitimate starting points for philosophy. The existence of other people, physical objects, and a world outside the mind need not be established from first principles before inquiry can begin.

Its force is methodological as much as metaphysical: philosophical theories should not automatically overturn deeply grounded ordinary beliefs merely because those beliefs cannot be proven with mathematical certainty.

Realism versus anti-realism

The contrast between realism and its philosophical opponents is often presented as a simple opposition to idealism—the view that gives a more fundamental role to the mind, experience, or ideas in determining what counts as reality.

However, realism also contends with a broader category of anti-realism. This includes nominalism (the denial that abstract universals exist), instrumentalism (the view that scientific theories are just useful tools, not literal descriptions of reality), and constructivism (the idea that categories of reality are socially or conceptually constructed by humans).

The important disagreement is therefore not merely whether people have experiences or use concepts. Both sides accept that they do. The deeper question is whether the objects, facts, and structures we experience have an existence or character that outruns human perception and conceptualization.

The central philosophical tension

Realism faces a persistent tension between two claims:

  1. There is a world that exists independently of our minds.
  2. Everything we know about that world is encountered through human forms of perception, thought, and language.

The first claim motivates realism. The second prevents realism from simply assuming that human knowledge is a transparent mirror of reality.

The most interesting forms of realism therefore tend to occupy the space between certainty and skepticism. They maintain that there is something to be right or wrong about while accepting that our access to that something is limited, mediated, and revisable.

In brief

Realism is best understood not as a single doctrine but as a family of positions united by a commitment to a reality that is not wholly created by our minds. Descartes makes the problem vivid by asking how certainty about thought can lead to justified belief in an external world. Plato locates reality beyond changing appearances; Aristotle grounds it in substances and their natures; Locke distinguishes objective features of bodies from perception-dependent qualities; Kant limits knowledge while preserving empirical realism; Reid and Moore defend the reality of the ordinary world; and scientific realists extend the question to the theoretical entities of science.

The enduring realist question is therefore simple to state but difficult to answer: what, if anything, remains true about the world when we subtract the contribution made by the observer?
