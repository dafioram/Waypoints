Eigenvalues

Eigenanalysis is only possible for square matrices. Singular value decompositions (SVD) are a more general factorization.

$\lambda_i$ is an eigenvalue and $x_i$ is the corresponding eigenvector for a matrix $A$ if

\[ (\lambda_i I-A)x_i=0 \]

the eigenvalues are found by solving the characteristic equation of $A$.

\[ \chi_A(\lambda) = det(\lambda I-A) = 0 \]

Or

\[ det(A - \lambda I) = 0 \]

Both lead to the same characteristic polynomial with the same roots. However, the first form is preferred since it leads to a polynomial with a 1 as the leading coefficient (called a monic polynomial).

Eigenvectors are non-zero even for eigenvalues with value 0 (Hill, 1996, p. 335). The magnitude of the largest eigenvalue is upper bounded by the row magnitude row sum for the row with largest sum of the magnitude of the elements, namely (Hayes, 1996, p. 48)

\[ \large |\lambda_{max}| \leq \max_i \left( \sum_j |a_{ij}| \right) \]

Identities (Noble, 1998, p. 292)

  • $eigval(A) = eigval(A^T)$
  • $eigval(A) = eigval(S^{-1}AS)$
  • $eigval(kA) = k \cdot eigval(A)$
  • For $A=B_1 B_2 \cdots B_n$ then $eigval(A) = \prod_i (eigval(B_i))$
  • $eigval(A^n) = (eigval(A))^n$
  • $eigval(A^{-1}) = 1/eigval(A)$
  • For $A=B_1 + B_2 + \cdots + B_n$ then $eigval(A) = \sum_i eigval(B_i)$
  • $eigval(A+kI) = eigval(A)+k$

Facts

  • If $\lambda = 0, \forall \lambda \in \operatorname{spec}(A)$, then $A$ is nilpotent.
  • If $|\lambda| < 1, \forall \lambda \in \operatorname{spec}(A)$, then $At$ is (exponentially) stable.
  • If $|\lambda| < 1, \forall \lambda \in \operatorname{spec}(A)$, then $\lim_{k \to \infty} A^k=0$ (stable in the discrete sense).

Multiplicity

The algebraic multiplicity of an eigenvalue is how many times it is a root of the characteristic equation above. For a given eigenvalue there are as many eigenvectors as its algebraic multiplicity. Of those eigenvectors the number of linearly independent eigenvectors is its geometric multiplicity. Therefore, the geometric multiplicity is always less than or equal to the algebraic multiplicity for an eigenvalue. When the multiplicities are equal all of the eigenvectors are linearly independent. Having linearly independent eigenvectors is a necessary and sufficient condition for the matrix to be diagonalizable (Hill, 1996, p. 344) (this allows the matrix of eigenvectors to be invertible in the similarity transform). The matrix having linearly independent eigenvectors is equivalent to it being diagonalizable. The number of Jordan blocks for a given eigenvalue is its geometric multiplicity.

If the eigenvalues are distinct (algebraic multiplicity is one for all eigenvalues) then the eigenvectors are independent [proof] (Hill, 1996, p. 346).

Symmetric matrices and Hermitian matrices are an example of a matrix that is diagonalizable even in cases that its eigenvalues are not distinct. An example of finding the eigenvalues and vectors of a 2×2 Hermitian matrix is given here.

Note: the eigenvectors being linearly independent does not imply that the columns or rows are independent. This is because the additional linearly independent eigenvectors are paired with eigenvalues that are zero, so that those eigenvectors actually span the null space. However the number of independent eigenvectors is always greater than or equal to the number of independent columns, so that independence of columns implies independence of eigenvectors (not verified).

Eigenvectors

While the eigenvectors that correspond to non-zero eigenvalues do span the same space as the range of $A$ they are not the same basis. The eigenvectors represent directions in which the matrix scales by a particular amount (eigenvalue). There is a vector space for each eigenvalue — if it contains more than one independent eigenvector then the basis for that space is not unique (every linear combination of them is an eigenvector). See the SVD for details.

In general the SVD cannot be used to give the eigenvectors of $A$.

Finding eigenvectors

The eigenvectors of $A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ are the vectors in the null space of $(\lambda_i I - A)$.

  1. Form $(\lambda_i I - A)$
    1. Inspection, or
    2. Perform row reduction on $(\lambda_i I - A)$, then do back substitution.
    3. Nullspace procedure.

Finding generalized eigenvectors (Boyce, 2009, p. 426)

  1. Find all independent eigenvectors. Let $m$ be the largest integer number of eigenvectors that are independent (the geometric multiplicity).
  2. Find the generalized eigenvectors by solving: \[ (A-\lambda I)x_{i+1}=x_{i} \] where $i > m$. It will be found that $x_{i+1}$ contains a multiple of $x_i$, i.e., \[ x_{i+1} = \left[ \begin{array}{c} a_1 \\ \vdots \\ a_n \end{array} \right] + c_i x_i \] the $x_i$ contribution can be dropped since it will reappear again when the diagonalization is performed. \[ x_{i+1} = \left[ \begin{array}{c} a_1 \\ \vdots \\ a_n \end{array} \right] \]
  3. If diagonalizing, the P matrix will have the eigenvectors and generalized eigenvectors found in the previous two steps as its columns. Diagonalizing in this way will lead to an eigenvalue matrix that also has ones above some of the main diagonal elements (Jordan form). The generalized eigenvectors are independent of the other eigenvectors so the matrix is invertible.

Identities

  • $eigvect(kA) = k \cdot eigvect(A)$
  • For $A=B_1 B_2 \cdots B_n$ then $eigvect(A) = eigvect(B_i)$
  • For $A=B_1 + B_2 + \cdots + B_n$ then $eigvect(A) = eigvect(B_i)$

Left eigenvectors

Left eigenvectors are vectors, $x_i$, such that

\[ x_i(\lambda_i I-A)=0 \]

which is the transposed system of the right eigenvectors,

\[ x_i(\lambda_i I-A) = (\lambda_i I-A^T) x_i^T \]

The eigenvalues and characteristic equation for the transposed system are the same. All the results are the same as the previous section except eigenvectors are being found of $A^T$ instead of $A$.
