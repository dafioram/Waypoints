Probability Calculators

Give it the probability of success (a die face, a loot drop, a coin) and get tables for planning and judging experiments. Everything updates as you type.

p can be a decimal (0.1667), a fraction (1/6) or a percent (16.67%). The extra margin and confidence add one more row or column to the tables.

1. How many rolls to measure p?

"I want to know the true rate to within ±1 point and be 95% sure. How many rolls?"

Formula: n = z² · p(1−p) / margin². Halving the margin costs 4× the rolls. Higher confidence means a bigger z, so more rolls (50% is much cheaper than 95%).

Both axes are logarithmic, so each straight line means rolls ∝ 1/margin². Dots mark 1 point (or your extra margin).

Uses the normal approximation. For very small p, keep the margin well below p, or the answer isn't meaningful. If you don't know p yet, p = 0.5 is the worst case.

2. What range of results should I expect?

"If the true rate is p and I roll n times, what observed rate will I get? What if many people each run the experiment?"

The observed rate varies around p with standard deviation σ = √(p(1−p)/n). The extremes across many experiments grow slowly, roughly 2.5σ at 100 experiments and 3.9σ at 10,000.

3. How small a bias can I detect?

"With n rolls, how far from p does the die's real rate have to be before a test would reliably flag it?"

The test: a two-sided test at significance level α. Power is the chance the test flags a bias of that size. Each cell shows the rates just detectable on the low side and the high side.

Normal approximation. Rates near 0 or 1 with small n are rough estimates.

4. Do two sources really differ?

"Die A gave 170 of 1,000 and die B gave 190 of 1,000. Is B really different, or is that noise?"

Each curve shows where that source's true rate plausibly lies. The more they overlap, the less you can tell them apart.
