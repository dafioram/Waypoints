The formulas, if that is all you need

These use the default convention: the array face is the x–y plane, z is the boresight, azimuth is measured in the x–z plane and elevation is measured up from that plane. Angles are az and el; u, v, w are the direction cosines along x, y and z. The u, v to az, el formulas are for a direction in front of the face, w ≥ 0.

Az, el to u, v, w

u = cos(el)·sin(az)
v = sin(el)
w = cos(el)·cos(az)
u = sin(az)
v = cos(az)·sin(el)
w = cos(az)·cos(el)

u, v to az, el

el = asin(v)
az = atan2(u, sqrt(1 − u² − v²))
az = asin(u)
el = atan2(v, sqrt(1 − u² − v²))

Cone angle, from the boresight

θ = acos(cos(az)·cos(el))
θ = atan2(sqrt(u² + v²), w)

Without scripts both variants are shown in turn: az first, then el first. With scripts, the one matching your convention is shown.

What u, v and w are


A planar array looks out from a flat face. Pick two perpendicular axes lying in the face and a third axis, the boresight, along the face normal. A pointing direction is a unit vector p. Its components along the three axes are u, v and w. They are called direction cosines because each one is the cosine of the angle between the pointing vector and one axis.


(u,  v,  w)=(cos⁡α1,  cos⁡α2,  cos⁡α3)(u,v,w)=(cosα1​,cosα2​,cosα3​)


Here α1 and α2 are the angles to the two in-face axes and α3 is the cone angle, θ, which appeared earlier and is explained more below. Because the vector has length one, the three components are not independent:


u2+v2+w2=1u2+v2+w2=1


Two numbers are enough. Give u and v and the third follows:


w=1−u2−v2(w≥0 in front of the array face)w=1−u2−v2​(w≥0 in front of the array face)


A target at range ρ sits at r = ρ(u, v, w) in the array frame, so u, v and w are also the position scaled to unit range.


The cone angle


The angle between the pointing direction and the boresight is the cone angle, θ, the α3 from above. Every direction with the same θ lies on a cone around the boresight, which is where the name comes from. Because w is the cosine of the angle to the boresight, w = cos θ, and what is left of the unit length is in u and v, so √(u² + v²) = sin θ:


cos⁡θ=w,sin⁡θ=u2+v2θ=atan2⁡ ⁣(u2+v2,  w)cosθθ​=w,sinθ=u2+v2​=atan2(u2+v2​,w)​


Use the atan2 form. It stays accurate for tiny angles, where arccos w loses digits, and it also works behind the face, where w is negative and θ is past 90°. From az and el there is a shorter route, because w = cos(el)cos(az) in both angle orders:


θ=arccos⁡(cos⁡(az) cos⁡(el))θ=arccos(cos(az)cos(el))


The cone angle is worked out from the direction, not chosen separately, so the calculator reports it and never asks for it. On the (u, v) disk it is the distance from the center: a circle around the center is a cone of constant θ, and the edge of the disk is θ = 90°.


The same angle goes by other names. Array texts often call it the off-boresight angle or the scan angle (or the angle off broadside), and antenna-pattern texts call it the polar angle θ. This page says cone angle throughout.


The visible region


For a real direction the square root above needs a non-negative argument:


u2+v2≤1u2+v2≤1


That is the unit disk in the plane of (u, v). Points on its edge graze the face: the cone angle there is 90°. A point outside the disk is not a direction at all; antenna texts call that region invisible space, and it is the hatched corners of the square plot above. If you want the direction behind the face, take the negative root; a fixed flat array cannot point there, so most radar software stays in front and this page treats the back as an option.


Antenna texts often reach u and v through spherical angles, u = sin θ cos φ, v = sin θ sin φ, where θ is the cone angle and φ is the angle around the boresight, measured in the face from the u axis toward the v axis. That is the same pair of numbers. Take care that this φ is not the radar azimuth. This page goes straight from az and el instead, and leaves spherical and polar conversions alone.







Az, el to u, v, w




Az first (the default)


Azimuth is the angle from the boresight toward +u within the u–w plane. Elevation then lifts the direction out of that plane toward +v. The part that stays in the u–w plane shrinks by cos(el), which is why both u and w carry that factor.


u=cos⁡(el) sin⁡(az)v=sin⁡(el)w=cos⁡(el) cos⁡(az)uvw​=cos(el)sin(az)=sin(el)=cos(el)cos(az)​






El first


Elevation is the angle from the boresight toward +v within the v–w plane. Azimuth then swings the direction sideways, out of that plane toward +u. Now u is simply sin(az), and v and w carry cos(az).


u=sin⁡(az)v=cos⁡(az) sin⁡(el)w=cos⁡(az) cos⁡(el)uvw​=sin(az)=cos(az)sin(el)=cos(az)cos(el)​




Both orders are shown in turn when scripts are off.


Both give a unit vector for any angles. In both orders w = cos(el)cos(az), so the cone angle is θ = arccos(cos(el)cos(az)) in both orders. Within the standard ranges, az first reaches behind the face when |az| > 90°, and el first when |el| > 90°. Sign flips act as the conventions section below describes: negate u for an azimuth flip, v for an elevation flip.


Where the formulas come from

Start with the boresight unit vector and rotate it twice. A rotation about +v by az turns the boresight toward +u. A rotation about +u by −el tips it toward +v. The order of the two rotations is the angle order.


p = Ry(az) · Rx(−el) · (0, 0, 1)
  = Ry(az) · (0, sin el, cos el)
  = (cos el sin az,  sin el,  cos el cos az)


p = Rx(−el) · Ry(az) · (0, 0, 1)
  = Rx(−el) · (sin az, 0, cos az)
  = (sin az,  cos az sin el,  cos az cos el)


Here Ry and Rx are the usual right-handed rotation matrices about the y and x axes, with y = v and x = u. Rotations about different axes do not commute, and that is the whole difference between the orders: tilt by el first and then turn about v by az, and you get az first; turn by az first and then tilt, and you get el first.

u, v to az, el




Az first (the default)


Elevation comes straight from v. Azimuth is the angle of the (u, w) pair, so it needs w, which you recover from u and v:


el=arcsin⁡vaz=atan2⁡ ⁣(u,  1−u2−v2)elaz​=arcsinv=atan2(u,1−u2−v2​)​


If you already have the full vector, skip the square root:


az=atan2⁡(u, w)el=atan2⁡ ⁣(v,  u2+w2)az=atan2(u,w)el=atan2(v,u2+w2​)






El first


Azimuth comes straight from u. Elevation is the angle of the (v, w) pair:


az=arcsin⁡uel=atan2⁡ ⁣(v,  1−u2−v2)azel​=arcsinu=atan2(v,1−u2−v2​)​


With the full vector in hand:


az=atan2⁡ ⁣(u,  v2+w2)el=atan2⁡(v, w)az=atan2(u,v2+w2​)el=atan2(v,w)




Both orders are shown in turn when scripts are off.


Here atan2(y, x) is the two-argument arctangent, which keeps the quadrant. Flips: negate the angle that was flipped (az for an azimuth flip, el for an elevation flip). The cone angle needs neither the angle order nor the flips: θ = atan2(√(u² + v²), w), as above.


Things that go wrong


    

  • Outside the disk. If u² + v² > 1 there is no direction. Reject it rather than clamping silently. A value like 1.0000000002 from rounding is fine to clamp to the edge.
    • 

  • Precision at the edge. Compute w as √((1 − r)(1 + r)) with r = hypot(u, v). Near the edge that keeps more digits than 1 − u² − v².
    • 

  • The pole. In az-first, at el = ±90° the direction is straight along ±v and azimuth means nothing (u = w = 0, atan2 returns 0 by convention). In el-first, the same happens at az = ±90°. Near the pole a tiny change in u or w swings the other angle wildly, which matters when you interpolate (below).
    • 

  • Ranges. Az first: az in (−180°, 180°], el in [−90°, 90°]. El first: el in (−180°, 180°], az in [−90°, 90°]. Angles outside those ranges name a direction that a canonical pair also names.
    • 

  • Behind the face. With w < 0, take the negative root. In az-first this puts |az| beyond 90°.
    •

Az and el are not one convention


“Azimuth and elevation” does not pin down the numbers. Four choices do, and the panel above lets you set each:

Only the first choice changes the structure of the formulas. The sign choices are simple: flipping azimuth negates u and nothing else, and flipping elevation negates v and nothing else, in either order (w never changes). The boresight choice only relabels axes: with x as boresight, (x, y, z) = (w, u, v) instead of (u, v, w). The numbers u, v, w are the same either way.


People say “az over el” and “el over az” for the two orders, but groups attach opposite meanings to those phrases (one describes how the gimbals stack, another how the angles are defined). This page names a convention by what it measures instead.

Sine space


Sine space is the plane of (u, v), and the visible part of it is the unit disk inside the square you have been dragging around. The square runs from −1 to +1 in both u and v; its hatched corners lie outside the disk. The name comes from the one-dimensional case: scan in a single plane and u = sin θ, the sine of the cone angle. In two dimensions the disk is the orthographic projection of the hemisphere in front of the array onto the array face. The 3D view above draws exactly that: the dashed shadow of the pointing vector on the face is the point in the disk.


Which way is +u? It depends on where you stand. Because (u, v, w) is a right-handed frame, standing behind the array face and looking out along the boresight with v up puts +u on your left. Standing in front, looking back at the face, puts +u on your right. Which side a plot is drawn from is a drawing choice, not a property of the array, so the plots on this page follow a “Viewed from” setting in the convention panel. It starts on behind the array face. Changing it only mirrors how the u axis is drawn; no number, formula or angle changes.

The two angle grids share one disk and one set of (u, v) points; only the labels of the grid lines differ. In az-first the elevation lines are straight and the azimuth lines bow toward the poles; in el-first it is the other way round. The dot is the same direction as in the 3D figures above.


Why arrays work in sine space


The steering phase is linear. To point a beam at (u0, v0), element n at position (xn, yn) in the face gets a phase


φn=−k (xn u0+yn v0),k=2πλφn​=−k(xn​u0​+yn​v0​),k=λ2π​


which is linear in u0 and v0. The array factor then depends only on the offsets from the steering point:


AF(u,v)=∑nan e jk [ xn(u−u0)+yn(v−v0) ]AF(u,v)=n∑​an​ejk[xn​(u−u0​)+yn​(v−v0​)]


Steering the beam slides the same pattern across the disk without changing its shape, until it meets the edge.


The beam is a constant width in u. For N equally spaced elements with spacing d (in wavelengths below, so d = 0.5 means λ/2) and uniform weights, the −3 dB width is about


Δu≈0.886 λNdΔu≈0.886Ndλ​


independent of where the beam points. In angle the same beam widens, because u = sin θ gives du = cos θ dθ:


u=sin⁡θ⇒du=cos⁡θ dθu=sinθ⇒du=cosθdθ


Δθ≈Δucos⁡θ0Δθ≈cosθ0​Δu​


For a beam that is narrow compared with the cone angle, that means about twice the broadside width at a cone angle of 60° and about 1.41 times at 45°. (The 1/cos θ0 step is my derivation from du = cos θ dθ, not a quote from a source; the figure below measures the widths directly.)


Grating lobes are periodic copies. When elements are spaced far apart the array factor repeats in u with period λ/d, so copies of the main beam appear at


um=u0+m λd,m=±1,±2,…um​=u0​+mdλ​,m=±1,±2,…


The visible region is two units wide in u. A copy that lands inside it is a grating lobe, and the condition to keep them out for scans out to a cone angle of θmax is


d≤λ1+∣sin⁡θmax⁡∣d≤1+∣sinθmax​∣λ​


At d = λ/2 the condition holds for every cone angle, which is why half-wavelength spacing is the usual choice.

It is 0.5% at 10°, 4.5% at 30° and 17% at 60°. At high elevation the cos(el) factor in az-first makes things worse: at el = 60°, a given azimuth moves u by only half as much as at el = 0. The Jacobian says exactly how much.

These are derivatives with respect to angles in radians. The determinant is the area scale from the az/el grid to the sine-space disk. It is 1 at boresight and falls toward zero at the pole (az first, el = ±90°) and at the edge in the az direction. The area element in sine space relates to solid angle by


dΩ=du dvwdΩ=wdudv​


so equal areas in the disk are not equal solid angles near the edge: a patch of fixed area there covers more sky.


Pointing error


If az and el each have an independent error, the error in u and v follows from the Jacobian:


Σuv≈J Σazel JT,Σazel=diag⁡(σaz2, σel2)Σuv​≈JΣazel​JT,Σazel​=diag(σaz2​,σel2​)


Going the other way, invert J. The calculator above does both and warns when J is singular, which is where an error in the angle you cannot observe turns into an unbounded error in the other. A convention-flip does not change the sizes of these errors, only the signs of the off-diagonal terms.







Interpolating a sequence of angles


You have beam positions or target directions at certain times and need values in between. The interpolation space you choose decides what the path in between looks like, and for wide moves the choices disagree a lot.


Pitfalls with raw az/el


    

  • The seam. Azimuths 355° and 5° are 10° apart, but their arithmetic midpoint is 180°. Wrap the difference first, or unwrap the whole sequence before interpolating.
    • 

  • The pole. Near el = ±90° in az-first (az = ±90° in el-first) a small move in direction is a huge move in the other angle, so an interpolating curve in angle space swings out and overshoots.
    • 

  • Non-uniform angles. Equal steps in az and el are not equal steps on the sky. The cos(el) factor makes the true angular step in azimuth shorter at high elevation.
    • 

  • Components, not angles, for sparse data. Interpolating w directly, or interpolating u, v, w and not renormalizing, gives a vector of the wrong length.
    •

Many samples, not two

Quaternions


A pointing direction has two degrees of freedom, but an array’s attitude has three: it can also roll about its own boresight. A unit quaternion holds all three with no singularity, composes by multiplication, and interpolates cleanly. That is why attitude code uses quaternions, and why az and el can be written as one.


Conventions used here


Hamilton quaternions, scalar first, q = (q0, q1, q2, q3) (the code calls the four parts w, x, y, z), acting as an active rotation of a vector, p′ = q p q*, with the conjugate q* = (q0, −q1, −q2, −q3). Libraries differ: some store the scalar last, and some (the JPL convention) use the opposite handedness, so convert before mixing code.


Az and el as a quaternion


Build the attitude from the same two rotations as before, about +v by az and about +u by −el, then multiply in the angle order.

q_el = (cos(el/2), −sin(el/2), 0, 0)      about +u by −el

Swing and twist


Many different attitudes point the boresight in the same direction; they differ by a roll about it. The swing is the shortest-arc rotation from the boresight to the direction, with no roll:


qswing=(1+w,  −v,  u,  0)2(1+w)qswing​=2(1+w)​(1+w,−v,u,0)​


The swing turns the boresight by exactly the cone angle: its scalar part is √((1 + w)/2) = cos(θ/2). (It is undefined when pointing straight back, w = −1. The code returns a half-turn about v there.) Compare the attitude from az and el with the swing and what is left is a pure roll about the boresight, the twist. It is zero when either angle is zero and otherwise:


ψ=2arctan⁡ ⁣(tan⁡az2 tan⁡el2)≈az el2ψ=2arctan(tan2az​tan2el​)≈2azel​


ψ=−2arctan⁡ ⁣(tan⁡az2 tan⁡el2)≈−az el2ψ=−2arctan(tan2az​tan2el​)≈−2azel​


Both orders are shown in turn when scripts are off.


So a gimbal that points at az 40°, el 40° has rolled its face by about 15° relative to the shortest-arc rotation, even though it never rolled a motor. It matters for anything tied to the face’s own axes, such as polarization, a patch of the array, or a camera on the same mount.

Interpolating attitudes


Spherical linear interpolation of two unit quaternions follows a great-circle arc on the four-dimensional sphere at constant angular speed, which is a rotation about a fixed axis at constant rate:


q(s)=sin⁡((1−s)Ω)sin⁡Ω q0+sin⁡(s Ω)sin⁡Ω q1,cos⁡Ω=q0⋅q1q(s)=sinΩsin((1−s)Ω)​q0​+sinΩsin(sΩ)​q1​,cosΩ=q0​⋅q1​


Two details. If q0·q1 is negative, negate one quaternion first, because q and −q are the same rotation and the long way round is almost never wanted. And when q0 and q1 are nearly equal, sin Ω is tiny; the code falls back to normalized linear interpolation there (a practical safeguard, not part of the definition). Slerp on attitudes interpolates the twist too, which is why the fourth path in the comparison above differs from the great circle.







Where this meets the rest of a radar chain


A tilted face and the platform frame


u, v and w belong to the array face. Targets are usually known in a platform or local frame (east, north, up, for example; the LLA and ECEF page gets you from geodetic position to there). If the face is tilted or rotated relative to that frame by a quaternion qface, rotate the direction into the face frame before applying any formula on this page:


pface=qface∗⊗pplatform⊗qfacepface​=qface∗​⊗pplatform​⊗qface​


Applying az/el formulas to platform-frame angles on a tilted face is a common source of errors that grow with tilt and look like scan loss.


Several faces


A multi-face system has one (u, v, w) frame per face. The face that sees a target is the one for which w is positive and large; the disk edge is where handoff has to happen.


Monopulse


Monopulse error signals are close to linear in the offset from boresight near the center of the beam. If you estimate that offset, do it in u and v and convert to angles last. I did not test this against measurements.


Left out on purpose


Antenna pattern and scan-loss modeling beyond the array factor above, polarization and roll conventions, atmospheric refraction, and spherical or polar conversions. The calculator and code assume ideal geometry in a single face frame.

radar_uv.py: conversions, Jacobian, quaternions, interpolation

About 150 lines. No dependencies except NumPy.


"""Radar az/el <-> direction cosines (u, v, w), with quaternions and angle interpolation.

Frame: (u, v, w) are the components of the pointing unit vector along the
array's first in-plane axis, second in-plane axis, and boresight.
Default frame: x = u, y = v, z = w (z is boresight).
All angles are in radians.
"""
import numpy as np

AZ_EL = "az_el"   # az is measured in the u-w plane (x-z plane), el is measured up from that plane
EL_AZ = "el_az"   # el is measured in the v-w plane (y-z plane), az is measured sideways from that plane


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- angles <-> direction cosines
def azel_to_uvw(az, el, order=AZ_EL, az_sign=1, el_sign=1):
    az, el = az_sign * az, el_sign * el
    if order == AZ_EL:
        return np.cos(el) * np.sin(az), np.sin(el), np.cos(el) * np.cos(az)
    return np.sin(az), np.cos(az) * np.sin(el), np.cos(az) * np.cos(el)


def uv_to_w(u, v, behind=False):
    """w from u and v. (1 - r)(1 + r) loses less precision than 1 - u^2 - v^2 near the edge."""
    r = np.hypot(u, v)
    if r > 1:
        raise ValueError("u^2 + v^2 > 1: outside the visible region")
    w = np.sqrt((1 - r) * (1 + r))
    return -w if behind else w


def uvw_to_azel(u, v, w, order=AZ_EL, az_sign=1, el_sign=1):
    if order == AZ_EL:
        az, el = np.arctan2(u, w), np.arctan2(v, np.hypot(u, w))
    else:
        az, el = np.arctan2(u, np.hypot(v, w)), np.arctan2(v, w)
    return az_sign * az, el_sign * el


def uv_to_azel(u, v, behind=False, **kw):
    return uvw_to_azel(u, v, uv_to_w(u, v, behind), **kw)


def cone_angle(u, v, w):
    """Angle between the direction and the boresight (also called the off-boresight or polar angle).
    atan2 stays accurate for tiny angles, where acos(w) would not, and it also works behind the face (w < 0)."""
    return np.arctan2(np.hypot(u, v), w)


def uvw_to_xyz(u, v, w, boresight="z"):
    """Same vector, labeled in the chosen frame: z boresight -> (x,y,z) = (u,v,w); x boresight -> (w,u,v)."""
    return (u, v, w) if boresight == "z" else (w, u, v)


def jacobian(az, el, order=AZ_EL, az_sign=1, el_sign=1):
    """d(u, v) / d(az, el), for the angles as the user states them."""
    a, e = az_sign * az, el_sign * el
    if order == AZ_EL:
        J = np.array([[np.cos(e) * np.cos(a), -np.sin(e) * np.sin(a)],
                      [0.0,                    np.cos(e)]])
    else:
        J = np.array([[np.cos(a),               0.0],
                      [-np.sin(a) * np.sin(e),  np.cos(a) * np.cos(e)]])
    return J * np.array([az_sign, el_sign])      # chain rule for the sign flips (scales columns)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- quaternions, [w, x, y, z], Hamilton, active
def qmul(a, b):
    aw, ax, ay, az = a
    bw, bx, by, bz = b
    return np.array([aw*bw - ax*bx - ay*by - az*bz,
                     aw*bx + ax*bw + ay*bz - az*by,
                     aw*by - ax*bz + ay*bw + az*bx,
                     aw*bz + ax*by - ay*bx + az*bw])


def qconj(q):
    return np.array([q[0], -q[1], -q[2], -q[3]])


def qrot(q, p):
    """Rotate vector p by unit quaternion q: q * [0, p] * conj(q)."""
    return qmul(qmul(q, np.array([0.0, *p])), qconj(q))[1:]


def q_axis_angle(axis, angle):
    n = np.asarray(axis, float)
    return np.array([np.cos(angle / 2), *(np.sin(angle / 2) * n / np.linalg.norm(n))])


def azel_to_quat(az, el, order=AZ_EL, az_sign=1, el_sign=1):
    """Attitude that carries boresight (0, 0, 1) onto the pointing direction."""
    q_az = q_axis_angle([0, 1, 0], az_sign * az)    # about +v: turns boresight toward +u
    q_el = q_axis_angle([1, 0, 0], -el_sign * el)   # about +u by -el: tips boresight toward +v
    return qmul(q_az, q_el) if order == AZ_EL else qmul(q_el, q_az)


def quat_to_azel(q, **kw):
    return uvw_to_azel(*qrot(q, [0, 0, 1]), **kw)


def uvw_to_swing_quat(u, v, w):
    """Shortest-arc rotation from boresight to (u, v, w): no twist about the pointing axis."""
    q = np.array([1 + w, -v, u, 0.0])
    n = np.linalg.norm(q)
    if n < 1e-12:                                   # pointing straight back (w = -1): any half-turn about an in-plane axis
        return np.array([0.0, 1.0, 0.0, 0.0])
    return q / n


def twist_about_boresight(q_attitude):
    """Roll of q_attitude about the pointing axis, relative to the shortest-arc rotation to the same direction."""
    u, v, w = qrot(q_attitude, [0, 0, 1])
    q_rel = qmul(qconj(uvw_to_swing_quat(u, v, w)), q_attitude)
    return 2 * np.arctan2(q_rel[3], q_rel[0])


def platform_to_face(q_face, p_platform):
    """Express a platform-frame direction in the array-face frame, given the face attitude q_face (face -> platform)."""
    return qrot(qconj(q_face), p_platform)


def slerp_quat(q0, q1, s):
    d = float(np.dot(q0, q1))
    if d < 0:                                       # take the short way round
        q1, d = -q1, -d
    if d > 0.9995:                                  # nearly parallel: normalized linear interpolation
        q = (1 - s) * q0 + s * q1
        return q / np.linalg.norm(q)
    om = np.arccos(d)
    return (np.sin((1 - s) * om) * q0 + np.sin(s * om) * q1) / np.sin(om)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- interpolating a sequence of pointing directions
def wrap_pi(x):
    return (x + np.pi) % (2 * np.pi) - np.pi


def lerp_angle(a0, a1, s):
    """Linear in an angle, going the short way across the +/-pi seam."""
    return a0 + s * wrap_pi(a1 - a0)


def lerp_azel(p0, p1, s):
    """Method 1: linear in (az, el)."""
    return lerp_angle(p0[0], p1[0], s), p0[1] + s * (p1[1] - p0[1])


def lerp_uv(p0, p1, s, behind=False):
    """Method 2: linear in (u, v); w is recomputed, so the result is always a unit vector."""
    u = (1 - s) * p0[0] + s * p1[0]
    v = (1 - s) * p0[1] + s * p1[1]
    return u, v, uv_to_w(u, v, behind)


def slerp_vec(a, b, s):
    """Method 3: great-circle (constant angular speed) between two unit vectors."""
    a, b = np.asarray(a, float), np.asarray(b, float)
    om = np.arctan2(np.linalg.norm(np.cross(a, b)), np.dot(a, b))
    if om < 1e-9:
        return a
    return (np.sin((1 - s) * om) * a + np.sin(s * om) * b) / np.sin(om)

interp_experiment.py: the spline comparison above

Needs SciPy and radar_uv.py next to it.


"""How much error does each way of interpolating a sparse angle sequence leave? (midpoint errors, degrees)"""
import numpy as np
from scipy.interpolate import CubicSpline
from radar_uv import azel_to_uvw, uvw_to_azel, lerp_azel, lerp_uv, slerp_vec


def angle_between(a, b):
    return np.degrees(np.arctan2(np.linalg.norm(np.cross(a, b)), a @ b))


def midpoint_errors(position, t_samples, t_query):
    """position(t) -> target position in the array-face frame (x, y, z) with z along boresight."""
    P = np.array([position(t) for t in t_samples])
    U = P / np.linalg.norm(P, axis=1)[:, None]                       # unit vectors = (u, v, w)
    truth = [np.array(position(t)) / np.linalg.norm(position(t)) for t in t_query]
    azel = np.array([uvw_to_azel(*u) for u in U])
    seg = lambda t: min(np.searchsorted(t_samples, t) - 1, len(t_samples) - 2)
    frac = lambda t, i: (t - t_samples[i]) / (t_samples[i + 1] - t_samples[i])
    cs_az, cs_el = CubicSpline(t_samples, np.unwrap(azel[:, 0])), CubicSpline(t_samples, azel[:, 1])
    cs_u, cs_v = CubicSpline(t_samples, U[:, 0]), CubicSpline(t_samples, U[:, 1])
    cs_vec = CubicSpline(t_samples, U)
    methods = {
        "Linear in az, el":
            lambda t: azel_to_uvw(*lerp_azel(azel[seg(t)], azel[seg(t) + 1], frac(t, seg(t)))),
        "Cubic spline in az, el":
            lambda t: azel_to_uvw(cs_az(t), cs_el(t)),
        "Linear in u, v":
            lambda t: lerp_uv(U[seg(t), :2], U[seg(t) + 1, :2], frac(t, seg(t))),
        "Cubic spline in u, v":
            lambda t: (cs_u(t), cs_v(t), np.sqrt(max(0.0, 1 - cs_u(t)**2 - cs_v(t)**2))),
        "Piecewise slerp":
            lambda t: slerp_vec(U[seg(t)], U[seg(t) + 1], frac(t, seg(t))),
        "Cubic spline on the unit vector, renormalized":
            lambda t: cs_vec(t) / np.linalg.norm(cs_vec(t)),
    }
    spacing = max(angle_between(U[i], U[i + 1]) for i in range(len(U) - 1))
    return spacing, {name: max(angle_between(np.asarray(f(t)), x) for t, x in zip(t_query, truth))
                     for name, f in methods.items()}


SCENARIOS = {
    "Crossing target": (lambda t: np.array([-4000 + 400 * t, 800 + 10 * t, 3500.0]), np.arange(0, 20.1, 2.0), np.arange(1, 19.01, 2.0)),
    "Overhead pass":   (lambda t: np.array([-1500 + 150 * t, 3000.0, 400.0]),        np.arange(0, 20.1, 2.0), np.arange(1, 19.01, 2.0)),
}

if __name__ == "__main__":
    for name, (pos, ts, tq) in SCENARIOS.items():
        spacing, errs = midpoint_errors(pos, ts, tq)
        print(name, f"(largest spacing between samples {spacing:.1f} deg)")
        for k, v in errs.items():
            print(f"  {k:48s} {v:8.4f}")
